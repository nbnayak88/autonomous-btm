A fictional case that shows the move from periodic, sample-based audit to continuous control audit.

Read this first. OmniVerse Energy Ltd. is a fictional company. Every number in this story comes from synthetic demo data built to show the method. None of it is a real result, and no real organisation is described.

Meet the company

OmniVerse Energy is an imagined integrated energy company with five plants, thousands of suppliers and one small internal audit team. For years its audit ran the way most do: once a year, pull a sample, check it by hand, write a report.

Act 1. The old way

Every year the team pulled 60 purchase orders out of 12,000 (half of one percent), asked the business for the paperwork, and tested each one by hand. The report said: no material findings. It took six weeks, and it was out of date the day it was signed.

Act 2. What the sample could not see

A random sample of 60 orders has a very different chance of catching each kind of problem. It is almost certain to catch a widespread issue such as payment without goods receipt (720 orders, about 98% chance). It has only about a 50% chance of catching an after-the-fact order (140 orders), about 11% for the 23 duplicate payments, and about 4% for the 9 orders approved by the person who created them. Sampling finds what is common. It misses what is rare, and rare is where fraud and serious error live.

Act 3. The wake-up call

A supplier phoned to ask why a payment had gone to a new bank account. Nobody in audit had seen it: the bank change and the payment sat in two systems and two reports. The board asked one question: how many more are there that we have not looked at?

Act 4. The shift

OmniVerse Energy moved to continuous control audit on SAP Signavio. The process is read from the event log instead of from a sample. The control rules run on every transaction. Evidence is kept by default. Each deviation becomes a task with a named owner, and AI only suggests, never decides.

Act 5. First full run (Procure-to-Pay)

All 12,000 purchase orders were tested. Only 71% followed an approved path. 1,075 orders (9%) failed at least one control test: 720 payments without a goods receipt, 23 possible duplicate payments, 9 orders approved by their creator, 140 after-the-fact orders, 31 split orders and 37 bank changes just before payment. The AI assistant pointed to one plant and one vendor group where most unreceipted payments sat. An analyst confirmed the cause. Nothing was closed without a recorded reason.

Act 6. One blueprint, nine audits

The same blueprint then ran on eight more audit areas. The table below shows each area: what the old sample-based audit did, what a full run on synthetic data shows, and the first action it points to.

Procurement (P2P) 60 purchase orders sampled; three-way match checked by hand 720 paid without goods receipt; 23 possible duplicate payments; 9 self-approved orders Block payment when the goods receipt is missing; check duplicates at entry
Finance (record-to-report) Year-end sample of journals; close checklist reviewed once 18 journals posted by their approver; 80 back-dated near close; 130 reversals without an original Lock posting dates after cut-off; second review for sensitive accounts
Revenue (order-to-cash) Sample of invoices matched to deliveries; credit blocks read from an exception report 180 delivered despite a credit block; 180 invoiced without delivery; 95 credit notes above limit Hard-stop delivery while a credit block is open

Act 7. What changed for the people

The auditors stopped collecting evidence and started judging causes. Process owners saw deviations within days, not months. The audit committee got a live view instead of a yearly report. The decisions stayed with people.

The story for each process

The same company, a different process each time. Each story has its own sample, its own wake-up call and its own numbers (synthetic).

Finance (record-to-report)

The old way. At year end the team sampled 60 journal entries out of 6,000 (1%) and reviewed the close checklist once. Findings arrived after the books were closed, so nothing could be fixed in the period.

What the sample could not see. Chance that a random sample of 60 journal entries contains at least one case:

Problem Cases in 6,000 journal entries Chance a 60-case sample catches one
Journal posted by its approver 18 16%
Unusual manual journal to sensitive account 55 42%
Back-dated or period-end journal 80 55%
Reversal without original 130 73%

The wake-up call. The external auditor found a large journal posted days after period close, approved by the person who posted it. Internal audit had missed it: posting dates and approvers sat in different reports and nobody had joined them.

The shift. Every journal is read as a sequence of events: create, review, approve, post, reconcile, close. Five rules run on all journals each night, so a late or self-approved entry is flagged the same week. AI suggests; people decide.

The first full run. All 6,000 journal entries tested. Only 76% followed an approved path. 642 (10.7%) failed at least one test, most often: 360 late close task; 130 reversal without original; 80 back-dated or period-end journal. First action: Block close until reconciliation is complete.

Revenue (order-to-cash)

The old way. The team sampled 60 invoices out of 6,000 (1%), matched them to deliveries, and read credit blocks from a monthly exception report. Orders that skipped a step rarely landed in the sample.

What the sample could not see. Chance that a random sample of 60 sales orders contains at least one case:

Problem Cases in 6,000 sales orders Chance a 60-case sample catches one
Credit note above limit 95 62%
Price change before billing 150 78%
Delivery despite credit block 180 84%
Invoice without delivery 180 84%

The wake-up call. A large customer on credit block received a delivery. Sales said the block was released by phone, but no release event existed. Finance learned about it only when the invoice went unpaid.

The shift. Order, credit check, delivery, invoice and cash are read from the event log. Rules catch delivery despite a block, billing without delivery and credit notes above limit on every order. AI suggests; people decide.

The first full run. All 6,000 sales orders tested. Only 84% followed an approved path. 802 (13.4%) failed at least one test, most often: 200 late cash application; 180 delivery despite credit block; 180 invoice without delivery. First action: Dunning and cash-application target by segment.

HR (hire-to-retire)

The old way. Payroll was tested for a sample of 60 employees out of 6,000 (1%) and leavers were reconciled once a year. A pay error could run for months before anyone looked.

What the sample could not see. Chance that a random sample of 60 employee records contains at least one case:

Problem Cases in 6,000 employee records Chance a 60-case sample catches one
Same person creates and approves a bank change 14 13%
Pay after exit 120 70%
Excess overtime pattern 150 78%
Hire before approval 180 84%

The wake-up call. Payroll paid a former employee for two months. The exit was recorded in one system and the payroll run read another. The yearly reconciliation would have found it, ten months later.

The shift. Requisition, approval, hire, pay change, payroll run and exit are read as one flow. Rules flag pay after exit, pay changes with no approval and hire before approval, on every record. AI suggests; people decide.

The first full run. All 6,000 employee records tested. Only 87% followed an approved path. 764 (12.7%) failed at least one test, most often: 300 pay change without approval; 180 hire before approval; 150 excess overtime pattern. First action: Make pay-change approval mandatory in the workflow.

Supply chain (plan-to-deliver)

The old way. The team observed stock counts and sampled 60 stock adjustments out of 6,000 (1%). Adjustments between counts were never systematically checked.

What the sample could not see. Chance that a random sample of 60 supply orders contains at least one case:

Problem Cases in 6,000 supply orders Chance a 60-case sample catches one
Stock adjustment without approval 90 60%
Long-idle stock 130 73%
Goods issue without order 240 91%
Frequent order rescheduling 360 98%

The wake-up call. A plant wrote off valuable spares with a stock adjustment and no second approver. It surfaced only at the year-end count, after the parts were gone.

The shift. Planning, ordering, receipt, production, movement and delivery are read from the event log. Rules flag adjustments without a second approver, goods issues without an order and orders rescheduled again and again. AI suggests; people decide.

The first full run. All 6,000 supply orders tested. Only 83% followed an approved path. 1,233 (20.6%) failed at least one test, most often: 420 late delivery pattern; 360 frequent order rescheduling; 240 goods issue without order. First action: Carrier and plant delivery-time targets.

ESG and sustainability

The old way. The sustainability report was sampled once, after the draft was written: 60 metrics out of 6,000 (1%) traced back to their sources by hand.

What the sample could not see. Chance that a random sample of 60 sustainability metrics contains at least one case:

Problem Cases in 6,000 sustainability metrics Chance a 60-case sample catches one
Metric with no source link 140 76%
Missing supplier declaration 160 80%
Late data submission 190 85%
Emissions figure changed after approval 300 95%

The wake-up call. An emissions figure in the draft report changed after sign-off, and the team could not show who changed it or why. The assurance provider asked for the evidence trail and there was none.

The shift. Each metric is tracked from capture to validation, approval, aggregation and report. Rules flag edits after approval, missing supplier declarations, late submissions and metrics with no source link. AI suggests; people decide.

The first full run. All 6,000 sustainability metrics tested. Only 84% followed an approved path. 772 (12.9%) failed at least one test, most often: 300 emissions figure changed after approval; 190 late data submission; 160 missing supplier declaration. First action: Lock metrics after approval; changes need a new version.

Customer experience (CX)

The old way. The team sampled 60 complaints out of 6,000 (1%) and read a monthly service-level report. The monthly average hid the slowest cases.

What the sample could not see. Chance that a random sample of 60 customer complaints contains at least one case:

Problem Cases in 6,000 customer complaints Chance a 60-case sample catches one
Refund without approval 180 84%
Repeated reopening 240 91%
Complaint closed without contact 300 95%
Complaint breaches service level 330 97%

The wake-up call. A regulator asked about complaint handling times. The monthly report showed a healthy average, but hundreds of complaints had run far past the promised time and many had been reopened.

The shift. Each complaint is read from receipt, triage and resolution to customer contact and close. Rules flag service-level breaches, repeated reopening, refunds without approval and closure without contact. AI suggests; people decide.

The first full run. All 6,000 customer complaints tested. Only 84% followed an approved path. 1,050 (17.5%) failed at least one test, most often: 330 complaint breaches service level; 300 complaint closed without contact; 240 repeated reopening. First action: Service-level alerts before breach.

IT (service and access)

The old way. The team sampled 60 change tickets out of 6,000 (1%) and ran an access review once a year. Emergency changes were hard to see among the routine ones.

What the sample could not see. Chance that a random sample of 60 IT changes contains at least one case:

Problem Cases in 6,000 IT changes Chance a 60-case sample catches one
Access not removed after exit 120 70%
Change released before approval 180 84%
Incident breaches resolution target 300 95%
Emergency change overuse 420 99%

The wake-up call. An emergency change reached production without approval and caused an outage. The review later found privileged access still active for people who had left.

The shift. Request, approval, test, release, access review and close are read as one flow. Rules flag release before approval, emergency-change overuse, missing access reviews and incidents past target. AI suggests; people decide.

The first full run. All 6,000 IT changes tested. Only 73% followed an approved path. 1,420 (23.7%) failed at least one test, most often: 480 privileged access without review; 420 emergency change overuse; 300 incident breaches resolution target. First action: Quarterly privileged-access review as a closure step.

Master data

The old way. The team sampled 60 vendor and customer changes out of 6,000 (1%). Duplicates and bank-detail changes are rare, so the sample almost never held one.

What the sample could not see. Chance that a random sample of 60 master-data changes contains at least one case:

Problem Cases in 6,000 master-data changes Chance a 60-case sample catches one
Master data created and used by one user 45 36%
Vendor bank change then payment 70 51%
Duplicate vendor or customer 140 76%
Unreviewed change to sensitive field 300 95%

The wake-up call. A duplicate vendor record, created and used by the same person, received a payment. The duplicate had been in the system for months and nobody looked at records, only at transactions.

The shift. Every master-data change is read from request and review to approval, update and first use. Rules flag bank changes followed by payment, duplicate records, self-created and self-used records and unreviewed sensitive changes. AI suggests; people decide.

The first full run. All 6,000 master-data changes tested. Only 89% followed an approved path. 552 (9.2%) failed at least one test, most often: 300 unreviewed change to sensitive field; 140 duplicate vendor or customer; 70 vendor bank change then payment. First action: Review step mandatory for sensitive fields.

Sample-based audit and continuous control audit side by side

Aspect Traditional sample-based audit Continuous control audit
Coverage A sample of transactions, often a small share Every transaction tested
Timing Periodic (quarterly or yearly), looking back Ongoing, close to real time
Detection speed Deviations found months after they happen Deviations flagged within days or hours
Evidence collection Manual extracts, spreadsheets, emails Automatic from the system event log
Process view Fragmented, one document or report at a time End-to-end view of how the process really runs
Risk approach Reactive: the issue is found after the damage Proactive: patterns and root causes show where to act
Control testing Tests the controls someone chose to look at Every control rule runs on all cases, repeatably
Effort High, repeated for each audit cycle High to set up, then low per cycle
Result A report with sampled findings A live view with a task and a recorded decision for each deviation
Role of the auditor Collects and checks evidence Investigates exceptions and judges the cause

Limits. Continuous auditing does not remove the auditor. A person still confirms the cause and decides what to do. It also needs good, trusted data and clear control rules before it pays off.
